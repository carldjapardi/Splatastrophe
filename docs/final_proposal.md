# Splatastrophe: Bringing Real-World Landmarks to Life with Gaussian Splatting (papers are linked)

If you've seen *San Andreas* (2015), you might remember the scene where the Golden Gate Bridge collapses during an earthquake. Inspired by scenes like this, we want to explore how computer vision can transform 2D photos of real-world landmarks into interactive 3D animations\! Our project, Splatastrophe, will use Gaussian splatting to reconstruct landmarks in 3D and let users interact with them by changing their appearance, adjust lighting, and experiment with physics-based effects like bouncing, swaying, and meteor strikes. Currently, methods like Gaussian splatting and COLMAP can combine photos taken from different angles into one 3D reconstruction, but the reconstructed objects cannot move on their own. Gaussian Splatting represents objects using thousands of tiny 3D particles called Gaussian splats. While the [PhysGaussian](https://xpandora.github.io/PhysGaussian/) paper can simulate physics on these particles, it does not automatically recognize different structural components. This makes it harder to simulate scenarios where a bridge deck sways while its towers remain stable, or where foundations remain anchored while other parts of a landmark move. To address this, we plan to adapt Professor Efros’ [Differentiable Blocks World](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.05473) paper to identify larger structural components and group the corresponding Gaussian splats so they can move together. We will then use [PhysGaussian](https://xpandora.github.io/PhysGaussian/) to simulate how these larger objects respond to forces like gravity and collisions. By combining these techniques in a new way, we hope to create more natural and creative simulations. We believe this project will succeed because many techniques we’re using have shown good results in published research. Above all, we want Splatastrophe to be a fun, interactive playground that users and potentially filmmakers can use to reconstruct and animate landmarks. We’ll use two publicly available datasets: the PhotoTourism dataset, which provides photographs of famous landmarks from different angles with estimated camera poses and 3D reference points, and Tanks and Temples dataset, which offers high-resolution footage of buildings, monuments, and sculptures with reference 3D scans so that we can evaluate accuracy with our reconstructions. Our first experiment will be to reconstruct a small structure using Gaussian splatting, manually divide its splat into a few components, test whether each component can move independently while the others stay fixed without creating visual errors (Nov. 13-16). If this doesn’t work (causes a visual error), we’ll need to simplify the component grouping. If our first experiment does work, we’ll select overlapping photographs from PhotoTourism and Tanks and Temples, use COLMAP to match features across images and estimate camera positions (Nov. 17\) and identify the landmark in each photo and remove the surrounding background using segmentation masks (Nov. 19). We’ll then use Nerfstudio to train a Gaussian splatting model that combines these views into a 3D reconstruction of the landmark (Nov 22). Once the 3D landmark has been reconstructed, we’ll use Professor Efros's [Differentiable Blocks World](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.05473) to represent the landmark as simpler geometric components, such as a tower's base, body, and roof, and then group the corresponding Gaussian splats so each component can move as a unit (Nov. 26). Next, we’ll use [PhysGaussian](https://xpandora.github.io/PhysGaussian/) to simulate how these components respond to gravity, collisions, and other forces. We’ll initially assign properties like stiffness and flexibility manually, then experiment with using a pretrained vision model to identify materials from photos and automatically estimate these properties, which will make our approach easier to apply to varying landmarks (Nov. 28). Then, if time permits, we’ll experiment with a ray-tracing lighting system to create shadows that change as the landmark moves (Nov. 29). We’ll then work on building an interactive website where users can rotate and recolor landmarks, adjust lighting, and trigger animations with the click of buttons with a detailed write-up of our methods, experiments, and results (Dec. 4th). We’ll precompute and save the physics animations rather than simulate them live, since our computing resources are limited (\$100 budget for renting GPU resources). The user will be able to rotate the camera and explore the landmark as it moves. We’ll measure success by evaluating the quality of our 3D reconstructions, how natural our animations appear, and the performance of our website. We’ll create a set of a few photographs not used for training and then compare them with snapshots of our completed 3D reconstruction taken from the same angles. We’ll measure how closely they match using PSNR (target \>= 15 dB) and SSIM (target \>= 0.60). We’re also aiming for at least 20 FPS on our website and at least two convincing physics animations without major visual errors. Our initial early experiment described earlier will provide an early indication of if independent component movement works, and we will be evaluating our approach during check-ins. As for what we’ve tried so far, we initially considered a project focused more on reinforcement learning but shifted to Splatastrophe to better align with computer vision and photography. We also discussed potential technical challenges with Professor Efros, whose feedback helped us refine our planned steps. While we haven’t run experiments yet, we plan to test our core idea in the next few days.
